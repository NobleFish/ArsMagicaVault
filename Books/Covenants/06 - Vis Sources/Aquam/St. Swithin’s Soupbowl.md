By Matt Sprengeler

The tale of St. Swithin’s burial is known by churchmen throughout
Christendom. The holy scholar wished to be laid to rest in a modest
grave, but when he died, well-meaning folk instead interred him in a
splendid tomb within Winchester cathedral. A downpour struck, and it
rained for forty days and forty nights until the saint’s body was moved
to a more humble resting place. Even now, the people of Winchester say
that rain on St. Swithin’s Day (July 15) is a portent of more rain to
come.

So it is with the lonely stone column called St. Swithin’s Soupbowl. As
tall as a man, it stands atop a bare hill, sporting no decoration save
rust-colored lichen. The column is crowned by an ancient stone bowl that
seems to be of a piece with it. In truth, the bowl was set there
centuries ago by fierce Viking raiders who originally carried it off
from southern England, and may have carried it to anywhere in Europe.
Whether Swithin himself ever touched it is unknown, but if it should be
filled with water by sunrise of his day, the liquid within becomes Aquam
vis by sunset. Filling the bowl with regular water results in some vis,
but if rain falls overnight and fills the vessel, it produces twice as
much. Moving the bowl from this spot breaks the vis-producing effect.
